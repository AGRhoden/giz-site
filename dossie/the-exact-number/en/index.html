The exact number

How a two-person studio in Brazil produced a liturgical book that the market said couldn't exist

How a two-person studio in Brazil produced a liturgical book that the market said couldn't exist



In early 2021, a graphic designer in Brazil was asked to solve a problem that had no precedent in the country's publishing history: produce a complete Roman Missal in Latin and Portuguese, nearly two thousand pages, with original illustration work, bilingual typography, and a format that virtually no Brazilian printer could accommodate. The budget was fixed. The team was two people.

The result, published in 2023 by Editora Permanência, is the Missal Quotidiano — a liturgical book that, by the standards of the Brazilian market, should not exist in its current form.

What makes the project editorially significant is not its ambition. Ambitious books fail routinely. What makes it significant is the method by which a series of genuine impossibilities was resolved — not through resources, but through a particular kind of disciplined improvisation that the design industry rarely documents because it rarely produces objects of this quality.


The number

Before a single typographic decision had been made, the project required a page count. This is a common editorial formality, but in this case it was genuinely unknowable. The format hadn't been defined. The typefaces hadn't been chosen. The leading, the margins, the column structure — none of it existed yet. And the content itself was in flux: sections would be added, others removed, with no clear accounting of the net difference.

The designer's solution was to borrow the number from the reference edition — the 1957 missal of Dom Gaspar Lefebvre, the Belgian Benedictine whose work had served as the textual and visual model for the project. That edition ran to 1,984 pages. The number was entered into the budget, understood by everyone involved to be provisional, and quietly treated as a kind of working faith.

Nearly two years later, when all the files were assembled — divided across dozens of documents, containing two languages, musical notation, tables, 170 illustrations, and a bilingual column structure throughout — the final page count came to exactly 1,984.

In a book of continuous text, a page count can be managed with precision. In a bilingual liturgical volume with fixed liturgical content, variable rubrics, full-page illustration spreads, and a two-column layout throughout, it cannot. That the number held is not a production achievement. It is something stranger than that.


Several typefaces were tested. The second option was selected: Garamond Premier Pro, used with subtly altered proportions. Were these adjustments merely technical — or part of the improbable arithmetic that ultimately brought the book to its predicted page count?



The format problem

The reference edition was printed in a format of 10.5 × 15 cm — smaller than most Brazilian commercial printing equipment is configured to handle efficiently. The first printer visited required a minimum run of 30,000 units and quoted a cost approaching one million reais. The second could approximate the format but the finishing quality was unsatisfactory and the cost remained prohibitive.

A priest of the FSSPX in the United States offered a referral: the same printer that had produced the English-language missal for the American market, located in India. After a series of negotiations conducted in what both parties recognized as mutually approximate English, a format of 10.5 × 16 cm was agreed upon, along with a specification for Bible paper — thin, low-opacity, warm in tone rather than white. The paper itself was sourced from Italy, imported in a dedicated lot despite the relatively modest run of 5,000 copies.

Quality control at this distance required an unconventional guarantee. The designer requested a physical dummy — a fully bound, unprinted sample that would allow assessment of the paper, the binding structure, the hot-stamping, the embossing, the three-edge gilding, and the four ribbon markers before any printed sheets were committed. The dummy arrived. It was, by the designer's account, immediately convincing.

The printer has since been used for a second edition. The relationship has become, in the language of production, reliable.


A working template was developed at the outset to guide the layout process, defining the dimensions and styles of every element.



Two families, one logic

The typographic structure of the missal was built on a constraint the designer imposed on himself: no more than two typeface families for the entire book. This is not an unusual principle in editorial design. What is unusual is what it required in practice.

The bilingual column structure placed Latin and Portuguese in continuous parallel. The standard solutions — bold and regular weights for differentiation, or the same face for both — were tested and rejected. Bold-regular pairings destabilized the page. Using a single face eliminated the visual distinction that, liturgically and practically, the reader needed.

The designer settled on roman and italic, but the italic presented a legibility problem at small sizes on Bible paper. The solution he arrived at has no formal name: he modified the italic optically, reducing its angle to a point where it reads almost as roman while retaining just enough formal distinction to signal difference. The resulting treatment is not a variant that exists in the Brioso family. It was created for this book and exists nowhere else.

The rubrics — the liturgical directions that appear in red throughout — required a different solution. Rather than adapting existing characters, the designer created a small set of dedicated glyphs: three characters that repeat throughout the entire volume, handling the liturgical notations that in most missals are handled inconsistently. The font was named Vobiscum.


The two typefaces were selected and presented to the publisher.



The Canon

The Canon of the Mass — the fixed liturgical prayers at the center of the Roman Rite — occupies a distinct structural position within the missal. It is not simply another section. It is the section to which all other sections are oriented, and the designer treated it accordingly.

While maintaining the same two typeface families throughout, the Canon received a visual treatment that separates it immediately from the surrounding material: the folios — the page numbers — shift to the same red used for the rubrics. A reader opening the book at any point can locate their position in the liturgical structure without consulting an index. The red folio is not decorative. It is a navigational instrument built directly into the typography.

This kind of decision — invisible when it works, disorienting when it's absent — is characteristic of the project's overall approach. The design is enxuto, a Portuguese word that translates roughly as lean, and that in this context means: nothing that does not serve a function, nothing that serves a function without also serving the page.


The Canon was given its own graphic system: the red band marks the variable prayer of the day amid the fixed texts; in the enlarged detail, a small Easter egg—Afonso’s face.




170 drawings

The illustration program for the missal derived from the same Belgian source as the text: woodcut-style engravings by René de Cramer, produced in the early twentieth century. The images were not available in any usable digital form. What existed were reproductions of reproductions — printed versions of printed versions, each generation accumulating damage and imprecision.

The initial solution was manual reproduction on tracing paper, a process that allowed the designer to correct the accumulated degradation while maintaining the visual logic of the originals. A collaborator was engaged. After approximately one year and twenty completed drawings, she left to enter a Dominican novitiate in France.

The designer purchased an iPad, learned the illustration application Procreate, developed a custom brush, and continued. Each image began as a photograph of the original, corrected in Photoshop for geometry and contrast, then redrawn in the application. The process placed scanning, reference, and production in a single device and a single workflow. The knowledge gained from watching twenty drawings be produced manually had given the designer enough understanding of what the images required to finish the remaining 150 himself.

Among the completed illustrations is the Presentation of the Child Jesus in the Temple, which depicts the infant in the arms of Simeon. During the period of its production, the designer's godson was born. A photograph arrived. The face of the infant in the engraving is the face of Afonso. The designer understood that this book would outlast its production context — that it would be used, as the Belgian original had been used, across decades and generations — and that the image would be there when the child was old enough to understand what it contained.

His daughter Cecília, then ten years old, illustrated the image of Saint Cecilia in the Kyriale section. During the illustration phase she also served as a critic of her father's work, identifying proportional errors that were subsequently corrected. She later illustrated a small book commissioned by the editor to recount the missal's journey from India — a journey that, owing to an unplanned rerouting, took considerably longer than anticipated.

What the project reveals

The Missal Quotidiano is not a significant design object because it is beautiful, though it is. It is significant because it documents what happens when a set of real constraints — financial, liturgical, temporal, and geographical — is met with a method that refuses compromise on the things that matter and improvises freely on everything else.

The typographic solution that doesn't exist in the font family. The page count borrowed from a 1957 Belgian edition and returned, two years later, exactly. The printer in India found through a priest in the United States. The illustrator who left for a convent and the iPad purchased in response.

These are not the decisions that design education teaches. They are the decisions that design practice requires — and that the field, in its preference for documenting outcomes over processes, almost never records.

The book exists. It runs to 1,984 pages. The number, it turns out, was never provisional.
