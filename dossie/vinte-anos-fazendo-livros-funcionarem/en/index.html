The discipline of returning to the desk the day after delivery.


Over any long editorial trajectory, there comes a moment when continuity becomes identity. When we relinquished the structure of a full office, the machinery gave way to a lean operation: just two people, two types of experience, with decisions passing directly through the hands of those who sign the work. It was not a rupture in method. It was a distillation of purpose: to stop being a traditional supplier and become a long-term editorial partner. In place of the old structure came Giz — a four-handed studio that, rather than narrowing its focus, widened it: not this or that, but this and that, and everything else that might come.


A decade later, the result of that decision is measured less by visibility than by the accumulation of hundreds of titles we produced and helped produce with our expertise. Thousands of pages. Books designed not to be admired in portfolios, but to function in circulation — carried in backpacks, read on buses, reviewed by influencers.


This stance comes closer to craftsmanship than to the mythology of authorship. The rhythm is continuous, almost indifferent to milestones. Monday begins again for everyone: for the athlete who lifted the trophy, for the boxer who won the belt, for the designer who just closed a bestseller. The next day, there is always another title on the desk. WhileS. The Ship of Theseuswas still on its way to China, others were already awaiting their turn to begin; when the intense production ofMissalcame to an end, the next day brought no pause, but continuity.


Perhaps this is the least visible aspect of editorial design: the return to normalcy after delivery. Without ceremony, without interruption.

After twenty years with books, what stands out is not a collection of high points, but permanence. And upon reaching this milestone, it is fair to ask what it cost — not to lament the account, but because work that gives so much of itself deserves to be looked at straight on, at least once.
