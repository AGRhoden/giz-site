The discipline of returning to the desk the day after delivery.


In any long editorial trajectory, there comes a moment when continuity becomes identity. When we gave up the structure of a full office, the machinery gave way to a lean operation: two people, two types of experience. Decisions passing directly through the hands of whoever signs the work. It was not a rupture of method, but a distillation of purpose: to stop being a traditional supplier and become a long-term editorial partner. Ten years after the ô de casa, came Giz— a four-handed studio that, instead of narrowing its focus, widened it: not this or that, but this and that, and everything else that came.


A decade later, the result of that decision is measured less by visibility than by the accumulation of hundreds of titles we produced and helped produce with our expertise. Thousands of pages. Books designed not to be admired in portfolios, but to function in circulation — carried in backpacks, read on buses, displayed in shop windows and even to serve as decoration.


This approach comes closer to craftsmanship than to the mythology of authorship. The rhythm is continuous, almost indifferent to milestones. Monday starts again for everyone: for the athlete who raised the trophy, for the boxer who won the belt, for the designer who just closed a bestseller. The next day, there is always another title on the desk. While S. The Ship of Theseus was still on its way to China, others were already awaiting their turn to begin; when the intense production of Missal came to an end, the next day was one of continuity. Nothing prevents celebration and pride in achievement. On the contrary, it is the driving force that propels us forward to face new challenges.


Perhaps this is the least visible aspect of editorial design: the return to normalcy after delivery. Without ceremony, without interruption.

After twenty years with books, what stands out is permanence, not a collection of trophies. And in reaching this milestone, it is fair to ask what it cost, because work that gives so much of itself deserves to be looked at straight on, at least once.
