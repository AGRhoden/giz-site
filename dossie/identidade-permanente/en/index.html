Designing for Contemplation at Editora Permanência


Antonio Rhoden, senior designer and art director


At a time when book covers are increasingly elaborate for immediate recognition, algorithmic visibility, and declining attention, the work produced at Editora Permanência in Brazil follows a markedly different logic. Under the art direction of Antonio Rhoden, the publisher has developed a body of graphic design that resists market aesthetics in favor of intellectual coherence, visual discipline, and contemplative rigor.

Founded in 1968 by Catholic writer and journalist Gustavo Corção, Editora Permanência has long occupied a singular position in the Brazilian publishing market. Its explicitly formative—rather than commercial—mission has shaped not only its editorial program but also its visual culture. Rhoden's role as senior designer and principal art director must be understood within this context: design not as persuasion, but as formation.


Design as intellectual mediation

At Permanência, the book is conceived as a spiritual and intellectual object before being treated as a product. This principle governs every graphic decision. Covers should not summarize or visually dramatize the content. They function instead as thresholds—structures that prepare the reader's attention before the first page is turned.

A clear example is O Reno se Lança no Tibre, a work examining the internal conflicts of the Second Vatican Council. Rather than illustrating ecclesiastical debate or Roman architectural symbolism, the cover employs a dramatic classical painting of martyrdom, rotated and cropped so that bodies collide diagonally across the surface. The metaphor is neither literal nor explanatory, but structural: physical violence becomes a visual analogue of theological and institutional rupture. The image does not interpret the argument—it situates the reader within its tension.


O Reno se Lança no Tibre: conscious metaphor and carefully articulated composition reveal the intention behind the visual construction.



Image as meaning, not as decoration

Images at Permanência are never neutral. Drawn largely from classical painting, they function as semantic material rather than decorative elements. Cropping is one of Rhoden's most precise instruments: figures are truncated, gestures isolated, focal points partially subtracted. The objective is not stylistic fragmentation, but controlled orientation of perception.

In Vendée 1793 – A Family of "Bandits", a historical narrative rooted in collective experience and memory, the cover deliberately preserves a dense, populous composition. Rather than simplifying the scene for clarity or visual impact, the design maintains its multiplicity, guiding the eye along a structured reading path. The viewer is led to encounter individuals, not abstractions, echoing the book's refusal to reduce history to concept. Here, design respects narrative complexity by resisting reduction.


Vendée 1793 – A Family of "Bandits": the composition returns faces to history, refusing to reduce it to abstraction.



Consistency without style

One of the defining characteristics of Rhoden's work is the absence of a fixed stylistic signature. No recurring layout formula. No ornamental system repeated across titles. What unifies the catalog is not style, but criterion.

This is especially evident in A Crítica do Amor Puro, perhaps the most controversial cover in the catalog. The image—an expressive, non-classical painting by Bernard Bouts—abruptly breaks with the Renaissance and Baroque visual vocabulary frequently associated with Permanência. Dark, distorted, and psychologically charged, the figure destabilizes expectations of beauty. Yet the decision is precise.

The book is not authored by Corção but critically examines his thought. The image, produced by an artist personally connected to him, reflects the intellectual severity and resistance that define both the subject and the thesis. Typography derived from the artist's own hand further reinforces this alignment. The result may repel some readers—but never misrepresents the work.


A Crítica do Amor Puro: the disturbing force of Bernard Bouts and the controversial nature of the cover converge in a visual choice determined by precision, not convention.


Against decorative prestige

Among new publishers working with equally demanding religious and philosophical material, a predictable visual solution often emerges: ornamental frames, heraldic emblems, aged textures, and typography coded as luxury. These covers frequently achieve an appearance of refinement but remain interchangeable—equally plausible as wine labels, historical fiction, or devotional editions, but never as identity.

The art direction deliberately avoids this path. Permanência's covers accept visual tension—and even discomfort—when required by the text. Success is not measured by immediate approval but by intellectual fidelity.


Refinement is not a visual costume but the consequence of fidelity to content.


The designer as mediator

Antonio Rhoden understands his own work as a mediation between text, image, and reader. He proceeds from the idea that graphic design has concrete responsibilities: it is design that shapes how a work is perceived and organizes the attention of those who read, and for this reason it must be proportionate to the weight of the ideas it presents. The catalog of Editora Permanência, which he designs, is where this conviction appears most clearly. Much of today's design is oriented toward speed, trends, and the need to appear different. These books show that it is still possible to treat graphic design as a form of thinking, more interested in deepening reading than in drawing attention to itself. For those willing to read at this pace, and also for other designers, there remains the proposition of a design that works in favor of the text and prepares the reader to understand it.
